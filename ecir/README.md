## Overview

The Certified Incident Responder, commonly known as eCIR, is a practical incident-response certification from INE Security.

It focuses on the skills required to detect, investigate, understand, and respond to security incidents in modern enterprise environments.

The current eCIR exam covers five main domains:

- Threat Detection & SIEM Operations
- Endpoint & Network Analysis
- Digital Forensics & Evidence-Based Analysis
- Threat Intelligence & Attribution
- Reporting & Communication

The exam is fully practical and places the candidate inside a realistic lab environment representing a corporate security breach.

Rather than completing a separate investigation report, candidates investigate the environment and answer scenario-based questions based on the evidence they discover.

I completed eCIR on **August 10, 2026**.

It was my first INE certification.

Before starting eCIR, I had already completed:

- CompTIA Security+
- Blue Team Level 1 (BTL1)
- Approximately six weeks of hands-on SOC internship experience

Because of that background, I was not approaching incident response for the first time.

For me, eCIR was mainly about going deeper into incident investigation and applying Blue Team skills I had already started developing.

## 1. Threat Detection & SIEM Operations — 20%

This domain focuses on using security telemetry to detect and investigate suspicious activity.

The official objectives include skills such as:

- Constructing SIEM queries
- Searching for suspicious activity
- Correlating logs from multiple sources
- Identifying Indicators of Compromise
- Interpreting security alerts
- Analyzing endpoint activity
- Recognizing evidence of initial access


The important skill is not simply knowing how to search a SIEM.

You need to know **what you are trying to discover**.

For example:

```text
Suspicious Alert
      ↓
Identify Host
      ↓
Identify User
      ↓
Search Related Events
      ↓
Correlate Activity
      ↓
Determine What Happened
```

### What I Learned

BTL1 and my SOC internship had already made SIEM investigation familiar to me.

At Cyberstone, I had already worked with alerts and raw logs and had experience comparing dashboard information with underlying events.

Because of that, I already understood an important principle:

> **The alert is the beginning of the investigation, not necessarily the conclusion.**

eCIR reinforced the process of using multiple logs and telemetry sources to reconstruct what happened around suspicious activity.

Endpoint & Network Analysis is currently the largest eCIR domain.

It includes areas such as:

- Endpoint telemetry

- Scheduled tasks

- Credential access
- SAM and LSASS access
- Credential dumping
- PCAP analysis
- Command-and-control communication
- Lateral movement
- Authentication anomalies
- Data exfiltration
- Unauthorized access


This domain reinforced why endpoint and network evidence should be analyzed together.

A single source may not tell the full story.

For example:

```text
Endpoint
    ↓
Suspicious Process
    ↓
Network Connection
    ↓
Remote System
    ↓
Authentication Activity
    ↓
Possible Lateral Movement
```

### What I Learned

One of the strongest lessons for me was that evidence becomes significantly more useful when it is correlated.

A suspicious connection becomes more meaningful if you know which process generated it.

A suspicious process becomes more meaningful if it immediately creates external network activity.

A login event becomes more meaningful if it is followed by unusual execution.

Instead of examining:

```text
Process

Network Connection

Authentication
```

as separate findings, the goal is to determine whether they form a sequence:

```text
Authentication
      ↓
Process Execution
      ↓
Network Connection
      ↓
Additional Activity
```

That is much closer to reconstructing an incident rather than merely collecting indicators.

## Network Analysis

Network evidence can reveal:

- Source systems
- Destination systems
- IP addresses
- Ports
- Protocols
- Authentication activity
- Data transfers
- Command-and-control communication
- Lateral movement
- Unusual connections

A useful investigation process is:

```text
Identify Hosts
      ↓
Identify Conversations
      ↓
Identify Protocols
      ↓
Apply Relevant Filters
      ↓
Inspect Suspicious Traffic
      ↓
Extract Evidence
      ↓
Correlate With Other Sources
```

One thing I learned is that packet analysis is much easier when you begin with a question.

Instead of opening a PCAP and searching randomly, I try to determine:

> What exactly am I looking for?

For example:

- A specific host
- An unusual protocol
- Authentication activity
- C2 communication
- File transfer
- Lateral movement

That makes the analysis more focused.

## Endpoint Analysis

Endpoint evidence can answer questions that network traffic alone cannot.

For example:

- Which user was active?
- Which process executed?
- What command was run?
- Was persistence created?
- Was a service installed?
- Was a scheduled task created?
- Was the registry modified?
- Were credentials accessed?
- Which external systems were contacted?
- What happened before and after the suspicious activity?

This reinforced the importance of moving between different evidence sources rather than relying on one platform.

One of the most useful habits during a practical investigation is avoiding random exploration.

```text

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
```

Before investigating deeply, I found it useful to understand the environment first.

Identify:

- Hosts
- IP addresses
- Users
- Systems
- Available evidence
- Network relationships

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
Workstation
 ↓

 ↓

```

Then, when new evidence appears, it has somewhere to fit.

Without that mental map, individual findings can become disconnected.

## Build the Timeline

A timeline is one of the most useful ways to understand an incident.

Instead of recording only suspicious artifacts, try to understand their sequence.

For example:

```text
Initial Access
      ↓
Execution
      ↓
Persistence
      ↓
Credential Access
      ↓
Lateral Movement
      ↓
Command and Control
      ↓
Impact
```

The exact incident may differ, but useful questions include:

- What happened first?
- What happened immediately before this event?
- What happened afterward?
- Did the same user perform another action?
- Did another host become involved?
- Was persistence created?
- Did the endpoint communicate externally?

Chronology can turn unrelated-looking evidence into an understandable attack sequence.

## Correlate Evidence

One of the most important skills I developed through BTL1 and reinforced through eCIR was correlation.

An individual artifact may provide limited context.

For example:

```text
Suspicious IP
```

is useful.

But:

```text
Suspicious Process
       +
Connection to Suspicious IP
       +
Matching Timestamp
       +
Affected User
       +
Persistence Artifact
```

provides a much stronger picture.

The investigation becomes more reliable when multiple independent sources support the same conclusion.

Many investigations begin with only one useful pivot:

- IP address
- Username
- Hostname
- Timestamp
- File hash
- Process
- Domain
- Alert

Use that as your starting point.

For example:

```text

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓
```

## Separate Facts From Assumptions

A useful investigation habit is separating what the evidence **proves** from what you **think may have happened**.

For example:

#### Fact

```text
HOST-01 communicated with an external IP.
```

#### Observation

```text
The communication occurred immediately after a suspicious process executed.
```

#### Hypothesis

```text
The connection may represent command-and-control activity.
```

The hypothesis should then be tested against additional evidence.

This matters in real SOC work because legitimate activity can sometimes look suspicious.

---

## Do Not Stop at the First Finding

Finding one malicious artifact does not necessarily complete the investigation.

For example, finding malware does not automatically tell you:

- How it arrived
- Who executed it
- Whether persistence was established
- Whether credentials were accessed
- Whether it communicated externally
- Whether lateral movement occurred
- Whether other systems were affected
- Whether data was exfiltrated

The goal is not:

```text
Find Malware
```

The goal is:

```text
Understand the Incident
```

My SOC internship exposed me to real alerts, logs, and operational uncertainty.

eCIR then helped me go deeper into answering questions such as:

> **What actually happened?**  
> **Which systems were affected?**  
> **How did the attacker move through the environment?**  
> **Which evidence supports that conclusion?**  
> **What happened first and what happened next?**  
> **How should the organization respond?**

The strongest skill I took away from eCIR was not one particular tool.

It was a more structured way of moving from:

```text
Alert
```

to:

```text
Alert
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Correlation
  ↓
Timeline
  ↓
Scope
  ↓
Impact
  ↓
Response
```

That is the role eCIR played in my learning path: taking the investigation foundation I already had and pushing it further into practical incident response.
